# Who You're Looking For

bits&bytes™ is not a resume collector. We want people who ship real things, own their lane without being told twice, and make everyone around them sharper. Age doesn't matter. School doesn't matter. The only question is whether this person raises or lowers the standard of the room.

You're not here to be impressed. You're here to figure out if they're actually built for this.

> 💡 **The one question you're really answering in every interview:** *Will this person make bits&bytes™ better?*
> 

---

# Before You Start

- Pull their application from the [👾 Join Applications database](https://www.notion.so/500459b5d81a4f939683f85005bcab61). Status should be **In Review** before the call happens.
- Read every field they filled out. Don't waste session time on things they already wrote down.
- Check their portfolio link, GitHub, or any file they uploaded under "Anything you want us to see?"
- Know what role they selected and what gap you're trying to fill.
- Have this guide open. Use the scoring rubric at the end.
- Keep the session to **30–45 minutes**. Respect their time — it signals how we operate.
- You don't need to conduct this alone. If a founder is on the call, let them take the harder questions. If you're solo, stick to this flow.

# Reading the Application Before the Call

Every field tells you something. Some things more than others.

**"What's the most random fact about you?"** — Read this one carefully. A boring answer isn't just boring — it means they either didn't try or genuinely have nothing interesting going on. Someone who writes something unexpected is showing you something real about how they think when there's no correct option.

**"Tell us about your experience in organizing events."** — Don't take this at face value. "I helped with our school fest" and "I coordinated 80 volunteers for a 3-day event" are both "experience" — probe the actual scope, their specific role, and what went wrong.

**"Why do you want to be part of this organisation?"** — The prompt literally says "answer like you're pitching to a reality show judge." If they gave you a corporate paragraph anyway, that's already information. They either didn't bother reading, or they have nothing real to say. Use whatever they wrote as a launchpad: *"You wrote X — tell me more about that."*

**Coding Skill (A/B/C/D)** — Don't screen people out on this. D ("What's coding?") in a Designer or Outreach role is fine. What matters is that their self-assessment is honest and matches what else they've shared. A mismatch is worth probing.

**"What's your role in a team?"** — Cross-reference this with everything else they wrote. Someone who picks "Programmer" but has zero projects to show is worth asking about. Someone who picks "Emotional Support" without irony might be your best ops hire — or they're not taking this seriously. You won't know until you ask.

**Age** — We only take highschoolers and droppers. Flag anything outside that window before the call, don't waste their time or yours.

**Location** — Relevant for in-person events and city-specific squads. Note it but don't filter on it unless there's a specific reason.

# Scheduling the Interview

Scheduling isn't just admin — it's the first real interaction a candidate has with how you operate. Do it fast, do it clearly, and never leave them hanging.

## When to Schedule

**Move fast. Top candidates have options.**

- Schedule within **24–48 hours** of triaging the application from **Inbox** to **In Review**.
- Speed signals priority. Delays signal disorganization or lack of interest — both kill momentum.
- If you know the application is strong after reading it, reach out immediately. Don't wait for a "perfect time."

**Best times for interviews:**

- **Mid-morning (10:00 AM – 11:30 AM IST)** — both parties are alert, settled into the day, and focused.
- **Tuesday or Wednesday** — avoid Mondays (people catching up) and Fridays (mentally checked out).
- Avoid lunch hours (12:00 PM – 1:30 PM) and late afternoons (after 4:00 PM) when energy and attention drop.

## How to Reach Out

Use **email** for the initial scheduling message. Keep it short, direct, and respectful.

**Template:**

> Subject: bits&bytes™ — Interview for [Role Track]
> 

> Hi [Name],
> 

> Thanks for applying to bits&bytes™. We've reviewed your application and want to speak with you.
> 

> We're looking to schedule a **30–45 minute call** to get to know you better and discuss what you'd be working on here.
> 

> Please share **3–4 time slots** that work for you over the next **3–5 days**. Include your timezone if you're not in IST.
> 

> Looking forward to it.
> 

> [Your Name]
> 

> bits&bytes™
> 

**What to include:**

- Expected duration (30–45 minutes)
- Interview format (video call via Google Meet/Zoom)
- What to expect: informal conversation, no need to prepare a presentation
- Ask them to propose times — don't force them into a single slot

**What not to do:**

- Don't send a calendar invite without asking for availability first.
- Don't use overly formal or corporate language. We're not a bank.
- Don't make them wait more than 2 business days for confirmation once they respond.

## Coordinating Availability

**Minimize back-and-forth. Respect everyone's time.**

- If they propose times that don't work, reply within 12 hours with 2–3 alternative slots.
- If scheduling with a founder or multiple interviewers, **block interview time slots in advance** so you're not scrambling.
- Always confirm the final time **at least 24 hours before** the interview.
- Send a **calendar invite** once confirmed with:
    - Meeting link (Google Meet preferred)
    - A one-line agenda: *"Informal conversation about your experience and fit for [track]."*
    - Interviewer name(s)
    - Reminder that it's 30–45 minutes

**Time zones:**

- If the candidate is outside IST, **always confirm their timezone** and double-check conversion.
- Write the time in both timezones in the invite to avoid confusion.

**Buffers:**

- Leave **10–15 minutes** between back-to-back interviews. Things run over. You need time to write notes and reset.

## When NOT to Schedule

Don't waste time on candidates who clearly aren't aligned. Screen these out before you reach the scheduling stage.

**Auto-reject if:**

- **Age outside eligibility** — we only take high schoolers and droppers. If they're in college or older, close it respectfully.
- **Application is empty or lazy** — one-word answers, no thought, clearly didn't read the prompts.
- **No relevant output and no curiosity** — someone applying to Tech track with zero projects, zero GitHub, and "I want to learn coding" as their pitch. Not ready yet.
- **Copy-pasted responses** — if every answer feels like it came from ChatGPT or a template, it probably did. Move on.
- **Red flags in tone** — overly entitled ("What do I get out of this?"), dismissive, or rude in written responses.

If any of the above apply, **update the status to Rejected** and send a brief, respectful close-out message *the same day*. Don't let it sit.

**Example rejection (pre-interview):**

> Hi [Name],
> 

> Thanks for your interest in bits&bytes™. After reviewing your application, we've decided not to move forward at this time.
> 

> We're looking for people with demonstrated output in [track], and we didn't see enough overlap with what we're building right now.
> 

> We appreciate you taking the time to apply. Best of luck with what you're working on.
> 

> [Your Name]
> 

> bits&bytes™
> 

## What to Avoid

**Common scheduling mistakes that hurt candidate experience:**

- **Ghosting after they respond.** If someone gives you their availability and you go silent for 3+ days, you've already told them how the org operates.
- **Rescheduling more than once.** Emergencies happen, but if *you're* the one constantly rescheduling, that's a red flag *you're* sending.
- **Vague invites.** "Let's chat sometime next week" is not scheduling. Lock a time or don't send the message.
- **Overloading the message.** Don't send a 4-paragraph email about the org, the process, what to prepare, etc. Keep it tight. They'll learn more on the call.
- **Ignoring no-shows without follow-up.** If a candidate doesn't show up and doesn't message, send one follow-up. If they still don't respond, close it out and move on. Don't leave it in limbo.

## If They Don't Respond

**48-hour rule:** If a candidate doesn't reply to your scheduling message within 48 hours, send **one brief follow-up**.

**Follow-up template:**

> Hi [Name],
> 

> Just wanted to follow up on my previous message. Still interested in chatting?
> 

> Let me know either way so we can keep things moving.
> 

> [Your Name]
> 

If they still don't respond after the follow-up, **move them to Rejected** and close it out. No hard feelings — they're either not interested or not organized enough to reply. Both are fine reasons to move on.

# Opening the Interview *(~5 min)*

Don't start with "tell me about yourself." You'll get a rehearsed answer and waste the first five minutes.

Start here instead:

- *"What are you currently building or working on? Not what you've done — what's live or in progress right now."*
- *"What made you apply to bits&bytes™ specifically? What do you actually know about us?"*

If they fumble either of those, you've already learned something useful.

# Core Questions — Every Role

These apply regardless of track. Ask all of them.

## 🔥 Grit & Drive

The most important section. Talent isn't the question — the question is whether they finish things when it stops being fun.

**1. Tell me about a time something you were working on completely fell apart. What did you do?**

Do they own it or blame the situation? Did they rebuild or quietly move on? If they can't think of an example at all, that's worth sitting with.

**2. What's the hardest thing you've had to push through to finish something — not the most difficult technically, but the most tempting to abandon?**

Vague answers mean they haven't actually been tested. The real ones are specific and slightly uncomfortable to say out loud.

**3. Have you ever committed to something and then realised it was way harder than you thought? What happened?**

Did they adapt, or did they quietly lower the bar and pretend nothing changed? Did they flag the problem or bury it?

**4. What does "done" mean to you? How do you know when something is finished enough to ship?**

They should have a real opinion on this. Perfectionism is a red flag. So is not caring. The person you want ships something imperfect and goes back to fix it.

**5. Tell me about something you built, designed, or organised that nobody asked you to.**

Clearest signal of self-direction there is. If they draw a blank, they wait to be told what to do.

---

## 🧠 Thinking & Problem-Solving

**6. Walk me through a decision you made that turned out to be wrong. How did you find out, and what did you do?**

Someone who can't admit being wrong is exhausting and eventually dangerous. You want to know they've updated before.   

**7. If we gave you full ownership of one thing at bits&bytes™ starting tomorrow — what would it be, and what's the first thing you'd change or build?**

If they're vague, they haven't done the research — or they're more interested in "being part of something" than in us specifically. Both are fine things to know before you go further.

---

## 🤝 Culture & Collaboration

**8. Describe a time you disagreed with a decision someone else made on a project you cared about. What did you do?**

Do they actually push back, or do they just comply and resent it later? Do they go quiet, or do they blow it up? You want someone who names the disagreement and works through it.

**9. What kind of environment do you work worst in?**

This question gets more out of people than "what's your ideal environment." Comfortable answers are useless — press for a real one.

**10. What's something you're genuinely bad at right now?**

If they can't name something real, they either don't reflect or they're managing the impression. Either way, not a great sign.

# Role-Specific Questions

- 🎨 Creative Track (Design, Content, Strategy)
    - Show me something you made that you're proud of. Why this one?
    - Talk me through a creative decision where you pushed back on a brief or changed direction mid-way. What happened?
    - How do you decide when something looks or feels "right"? What's your internal standard?
    - What does your creative process actually look like on a bad day — when you're stuck?
    - What's a piece of work you've seen recently that you wish you'd made? Why?
- 💻 Tech Track (Dev, AI, Systems, Infra)
    - Walk me through the most technically constrained project you've shipped. What broke, and how did you fix it?
    - What's something you built that failed in production or in front of real users? What did that teach you?
    - How do you decide when to build vs. use an existing tool or library?
    - What's in your current stack? What would you change about it, and why?
    - What are you currently learning, and what made you decide to learn it?
- 📣 Outreach & Social Track
    - Show me a campaign or post you've run. Walk me through why you made the choices you made.
    - How do you measure whether your outreach or content actually worked?
    - What's the biggest community or audience-building mistake you've made?
    - How do you pitch something to someone who doesn't know or care about us yet?
    - What would you do in the first two weeks of owning our social channels?
- ⚙️ Operations & Comms Track
    - Tell me about a time you had to coordinate between people who weren't communicating. How did you fix it?
    - What systems or tools do you use to keep yourself and others on track?
    - How do you handle it when someone on the team isn't pulling their weight?
    - Walk me through how you'd plan and run a 100-person event from scratch. What breaks first?
    - What's the most overlooked thing in running events or organisations that most people underestimate?

# 🟢 Green Flags / 🔴 Red Flags

| 🟢 Strong Signal | 🔴 Concern |
| --- | --- |
| Has built or shipped something real | Only talks about what they *want* to do |
| Specific answers with real detail | Vague, rehearsed, or generic responses |
| Owns their failures without excuses | Blames others or the situation |
| Has strong opinions, holds them loosely | Either no opinions or completely inflexible |
| Asks sharp questions about B&B | No questions, or questions about perks first |
| Can work without being micromanaged | Needs constant validation or hand-holding |
| Has done something without being asked | Only acts when assigned |
| Talks honestly about failure | Avoids or deflects questions about failure |
| Uncomfortable with comfort | Optimises for safety and stability |

# Scoring Rubric

Score the candidate on each dimension after the interview. Whole numbers only.

| Dimension | 1 — Weak | 2 — Developing | 3 — Solid | 4 — Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grit & Drive** | Gives up when things get hard | Shows persistence sometimes | Has pushed through real difficulty | Track record of finishing what others would abandon |
| **Agency** | Waits to be told what to do | Occasionally self-starts | Proactively owns tasks | Relentlessly self-directed; ships without being asked |
| **Skill / Track Fit** | No relevant skill or output | Basic awareness, limited output | Has built or created real things | Strong output with real-world impact |
| **Communication** | Vague, disorganised | Communicates but imprecise | Clear and structured | Sharp, concise, knows when to stop talking |
| **Culture Fit** | Doesn't align with B&B values | Neutral; needs more context | Gets it; buys into the mission | Would make the team sharper just by being in the room |

# Decision Framework

| Score | Signal | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **17–20** | Strong Yes | Move to onboarding |
| **13–16** | Lean Yes | One follow-up task or call to confirm |
| **9–12** | Hold | Specific concern — document it and flag to Srishti |

| **Below 5** | No | Not aligned; close respectfully and promptly |

> ⚠️ **If your gut says no but the score says yes — trust your gut and document why. The rubric is a tool, not the verdict.**

# Closing the Interview *(~5 min)*

Always leave room for their questions. What they ask tells you as much as their answers did.

**Good signs:**

- *"What does success look like in this role in the first 30 days?"*
- *"What's the hardest part of working at bits&bytes™?"*
- *"What are you building towards as an organisation?"*

**Not good signs:**

- *"What do I get out of this?"* — as the opening question, before they've understood anything about us
- No questions at all. Means they either checked out or didn't care enough to think about it.

End with: *"We'll be in touch within [X] days. We move fast, and we'll be honest either way."*

Then actually do it.
